Beijing, April 19, 2026,
7:30 in the morning.
A 1.69-metre humanoid robot sets off on a half marathon.
It crosses the line in 50 minutes
and 26 seconds.
The human world record,
set a month earlier in Lisbon,
is 57 minutes and 20 seconds.
A year earlier,
in the same race,
the winner had taken 2 hours 40.
This robot doesn't come out of
Silicon Valley. It was designed by
Honor, a Chinese smartphone maker.
For 40 years,
we kept saying that China copied.
Today, it's the country we watch to guess what comes next.
To understand how we got
here, we have to go back to a country
where people lived on less than half a dollar a day.
1978.
According to the World Bank,
income per person in China is
157 dollars a year.
That year, Deng Xiaoping launches the policy
of reform and opening up.
The Party keeps power,
but it lets the market in through
small doors.
The first one opens in 1980,
right across from Hong Kong:
the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone.
In 1979, about 300,000 people live
in this area.
By the end of 2025, there are over 18 million.
Shenzhen makes things for others first,
then for itself.
Huawei, Tencent and BYD were born there.
In 40 years,
nearly 800 million Chinese people
have escaped extreme poverty.
That's almost three quarters of the fall
in poverty worldwide over that period.
And in 2025,
income per person tops 13,800 dollars.
Nearly 90 times the 1978 figure.
For a long time, the West reassured itself with
a simple idea: China manufactures,
but it doesn't invent.
January 2025.
A young company from Hangzhou,
DeepSeek, releases an AI model called R1.
It holds its own against the best American models.
DeepSeek says it spent about 5.6 million
dollars of computing on its base
model, a figure some analysts dispute.
All of it on chips restricted
by American export controls.
On Monday, January 27,
Nvidia loses about 589 billion dollars in market value.
No company had ever lost that much in
a single day.
This shock didn't come out of nowhere.
Australia's ASPI institute tracks 74 critical technologies.
In the most-cited research published between
2021 and 2025,
China leads in 69 of them.
In the mid-2000s,
it led in just 3.
But AI without electricity
is just a program waiting to run.
And on electricity,
the gap is dizzying.
In 2025, China connected more than
430 gigawatts of wind and solar.
In a single year.
For the first time,
wind and solar overtake
thermal power plants in its power mix.
Its renewable output reaches about 4,000
billion kilowatt-hours.
More than all the electricity used by
the 27 countries of the European Union.
That electricity then has to be stored.
According to the International Energy Agency,
more than 80% of the battery
cells produced worldwide in 2025
came from China.
And for the materials inside
those batteries, its share is even higher.
From batteries to cars,
it's only a short step.
In 2025, nearly 3 out of 4 electric cars
produced in the world
came out of Chinese factories.
That same year,
BYD, a company founded in Shenzhen in
1995 to make batteries,
sold 2.26 million fully electric cars.
Tesla delivered 1.64 million.
For the first time over a full
year, the American company was overtaken.
In the factories,
machines are taking over too.
In 2024, China installed 295,000
industrial robots.
More than one in every two installed on the planet.
And for the first time,
most of them came from Chinese makers.
The half-marathon humanoids are only
the shop window of this shift.
American investor Ray Dalio studied five
centuries of empires:
the Dutch Republic, then Britain,
then the United States.
For him, power follows a big cycle.
You rise by educating,
innovating, producing.
Then come debt,
internal divisions,
and decline.
In his 2021 book,
he describes a rising power,
China, challenging the established one.
Still, nothing is set in stone.
China's population shrank again in
2025, real estate has struggled,
and trade tensions keep piling up.
But one thing has already shifted.
The country once accused of copying
has become the one others study.
